Giving a Model a Pen: A Negative Result on Turtle Graphics as a Visual-Reasoning Probe

Naman Dhakad

Independent researcher · naman.is-a.dev · github.com/namandhakad712/drape

Abstract

We test whether requiring a language model to reproduce an image through imperative relative commands — a turtle with a position and a heading — rather than declarative markup, exposes spatial state tracking. It does not, and we identify four reasons why, three of which are general to the design of capability probes and one of which is specific and fatal.

First, the test items are degenerate: a 199-command spiral is reproduced to within 0.27% of its diameter by an eight-parameter program, with full coverage. Second, degeneracy has two independent doors — cheap programs and cheap absolute moves — of which only the first is normally guarded; six of nine shape families fail the two-sided test, and a regular zigzag is drawn by a single absolute move. Third, degeneracy is not an intrinsic property of a shape but a joint property of the shape, the grading tolerance, and the search class; the same zigzag requires 199 absolute moves at 1% tolerance and 1 move at 2%, so the threshold chosen by the experimenter determines the verdict. Fourth, and decisively, every quantity the task requires is either locally readable from the image or recomputable by summing the model's own emitted tokens, so no item design forces the computation into a non-textual channel.

We conclude that this route cannot answer whether a model reasons visually. We give a five-rule framework for testing whether a proposed probe can work at all before any model is called, and we report four bugs found in our own measurement code by the floor-check discipline that framework prescribes — all four of which had made the headline result look stronger than it was.

1. Introduction

A recurring ambition in the evaluation of language models is to measure not what a model says but how it represents. One prominent line of interpretability work argues that models maintain a limited internal workspace, and reports that in at least one frontier model this workspace "is built almost entirely out of words" [1]. If a model's internal representations are largely linguistic, then tasks that appear to require spatial or visual competence may be solved by translating the problem into language and reasoning there.

This raises a natural evaluation question: can we construct a task that forces spatial computation to happen as spatial computation, rather than as language? If such a task existed, a model's performance on it would be evidence about the format of its representations rather than only about their content.

This paper reports an attempt to build one such task, and its failure. The proposal was to use Turtle Graphics [2] as the output modality. Declarative vector formats such as SVG are order-independent lists of absolute coordinates. Turtle commands are relative and order-dependent: the pen has a position and a heading, and each command is interpreted against accumulated state. The hypothesis was that this makes state tracking obligatory, and that measuring how the accumulated state degrades over a long program would therefore measure something about spatial representation.

We built the interpreter and the analysis harness, and then — before calling any model — we tested whether the instrument could measure what it claimed to. It could not. This paper reports what we found, because the analysis transfers to other proposed probes and because the failure mode is instructive.

Our contributions are:

  • A demonstration that the proposed item family is degenerate: a 199-command shape is reproduced by eight numbers to 0.27% of its diameter (§5.1).
  • The identification of a second, independent route by which a proposed probe can be bypassed — absolute actions — and a two-sided validity test that catches both (§4.2, §5.2).
  • The observation that degeneracy is a joint property of item, tolerance and search class, with a demonstration that a single threshold change moves a shape from 199 required actions to 1 (§5.3).
  • A scaling test that is robust to threshold choice, and the observation that the fixed-tolerance test can only ever return a lower bound (§5.4, §5.5).
  • A structural argument that the probe cannot measure its target, and a five-rule framework for establishing this class of result before spending compute (§6, §7).

2. Background and related work

2.1 Turtle Graphics

Turtle graphics originated in the LOGO programming language as a way to give children a concrete model of geometric procedure [2]. The turtle is a drawing agent with a position and a heading; commands such as forward and right are relative to the current heading, and the agent's state is the accumulation of all preceding commands. This is the property that motivated the present work: unlike a coordinate list, a turtle program cannot be interpreted out of order.

2.2 Spatial reasoning evaluation

A substantial body of work evaluates spatial reasoning in multimodal models, typically through question answering over scenes and spatial relations [3]. These benchmarks measure whether a model produces correct spatial answers. Our question is different and complementary: not whether a model gets the right answer, but whether the task can be constructed so that the required computation cannot be performed in language.

2.3 Vector graphics generation

Generating SVG from images and text is well studied, with benchmarks covering a range of vector styles and evaluation showing that pixel-based metrics do not capture vector-specific qualities [4]. This literature establishes that models can produce vector output and that evaluating it is non-trivial. It does not address whether a particular output format constrains the internal computation, which is our concern.

2.4 Benchmark validity

That benchmark performance can fail to generalise is well documented; classifiers that appear robust on a held-out set can degrade on freshly collected data from the same distribution [5]. Our findings are of a related but distinct kind: not that performance fails to transfer, but that the item itself can be solved by a route that bypasses the mechanism the item was designed to measure. We argue in §7 that this failure mode is checkable in advance.

3. The proposed probe

The design under test was as follows. A shape is generated procedurally from a random seed and rendered as an image. A model is given the image and asked to emit a turtle program that reproduces it. The program is parsed against a whitelist and replayed in a simulator; model output is never executed. Two families of metrics are computed: fidelity (how closely the produced path matches the target) and process (whether the model used absolute commands, and where in the program they occur).

The intended measurement was drift: the growing divergence between the turtle's true state and the state implied by the model's program, together with whether the model ever corrects. The hypothesis was that drift would reveal degradation of an internal spatial state estimate, and that correction events would reveal whether the model monitors that estimate.

Six conditions were planned, varying whether an image was supplied, whether absolute commands were permitted, and whether intermediate reasoning was permitted in the output. The pre-registered outcome table is reproduced in §7.4.

4. A framework for testing whether a probe can work

Before running any model we asked a prior question: could the item be solved by a route that does not involve the mechanism under test? This section defines the tests; §5 reports what they found.

4.1 Test A: is the item expressible by a small program?

If a low-parameter program reproduces the item, then a model that recognises the shape's family can emit that program without tracking anything. We fit a hierarchy of state-free programs to the target: step length and turn angle modelled as polynomials of the command index, and as periodic sequences of varying period. A program is state-free if each command is a function of the index alone, so no accumulated state is consulted.

4.2 Test B: is the item expressible by a few absolute moves?

A model may bypass accumulated state entirely by emitting absolute moves: read a landmark from the image, jump to it, repeat. We measure the cost of this route as the fewest straight-line chords that remain within a tolerance of the true path, computed by greedy chord extension. Each chord corresponds to one absolute move. An item is valid only if it is expensive to draw by both routes.

4.3 The scaling test

Tests A and B both depend on a tolerance, which the experimenter chooses. The scaling test removes that dependence by asking not whether a cheap program fits, but how the required fit cost scales with the length of the shape. A shape whose fit cost is constant in the number of segments has a name; a model that recognises the name reproduces it at any length. A valid item requires fit cost to grow with N.

4.4 The floor check

Every metric in this paper is validated by feeding it the exact answer — the program that generated the shape — and asserting that it scores zero. This is not a formality. Four bugs in our own measurement code were found this way, all of which had made the results look better than they were (Appendix A). The floor check runs on every execution and the harness exits non-zero if it fails.

5. Results

5.1 The item family is degenerate

Our flagship item was a 199-command Archimedean spiral. It is reproduced by eight numbers.

The reason is that the spiral's kinematics are nearly closed-form. Turn angle per step has a coefficient of variation of 3.74%, and step length is very nearly a smooth function of the command index. Two low-order polynomials therefore carry the entire shape, and the resulting program is a loop with a counter that consults no accumulated state.

Table 1 extends this to nine shape families spanning analytic, periodic and random constructions. Six are reproduced by programs of at most 24 parameters.

Smooth analytic curves are exactly the shapes that admit compact closed forms, which is why they are easy to draw and why they are useless as probes.

5.2 Degeneracy has two doors

The second door is the more dangerous of the two, because it bypasses the task with no loss of fidelity. A model that reads landmarks from the image and jumps between them produces a correct drawing while accumulating no state whatsoever, and therefore produces no drift to measure. Six of the nine families in Table 1 are dead on this test alone.

Both tests depend on the grading tolerance, which is chosen by the experimenter. Table 2 varies only that threshold.

A regular zigzag — a shape that presents as high-frequency, many-turn and visually intricate — requires 199 absolute moves at 1% tolerance and one at 2%, because at 2% of its diameter it is a straight line. The question "is this item degenerate?" therefore has no answer in isolation. The answerable question is "at this tolerance, under this search class, is it?", and both parameters are chosen by the person doing the measuring.

5.4 The fixed-tolerance test can only return a lower bound

Our fitter searches a deliberately small class. A shape outside that class will be scored as valid even when it is a closed form. In Table 1, the varying curvature family scores as valid with 54.51% error — but it was generated by right(6 + 4·sin(2πi/60)); forward(9), a three-constant closed form that our fitter cannot see because a sinusoid is neither polynomial nor periodic at the sampled resolution.

The verdict is therefore always "no cheap program found", never "no cheap program exists". Widen the class and more items die. Item validity rests on an assumption that cannot be proved, only failed to be disproved.

5.5 The scaling test

The scaling test is the only test in this paper that is not an artefact of a threshold we chose. Its result is unambiguous: the same four parameters draw the spiral whether it has 25 segments or 200. A shape whose fit cost is constant in N has a name, and a model that recognises the name reproduces it exactly at any length.

6. Why the probe cannot measure its target

The findings above are correctable. Item families can be replaced, tolerances pre-registered, search classes widened. The finding in this section is not correctable, and it is the reason the line of enquiry is closed.

6.1 The accumulation happens in the turtle, not in the model

To emit the command for segment k, a model needs the turn from segment k−1 to segment k. This is the angle between two adjacent pieces of the picture: a local quantity that can be read directly from the image. The model does not need to know its own heading, because the turtle's heading is ground truth and the turtle maintains it.

It follows that a misread bend does not corrupt the model's estimate of anything. It corrupts the turtle's position, downstream, because the turtle integrated the error. The accumulation happens in the turtle, not in the model.

Consequence

The drift metric does not measure state tracking. It measures per-step angular precision — a perceptual acuity measure — because the state it appears to track is maintained by the simulator, not by the subject.

6.2 State knowledge is optional, and the escape hatch is always open

A model is forced to know its own state in exactly three situations: when it must close a path back onto its start, when it must place a later stroke at a location defined relative to the start, and when it must avoid crossing a stroke it has already drawn. In all three it can escape by reading the target off the image and using an absolute move — which is the second door of §5.2.

State tracking is therefore required only when absolute commands are forbidden and the task simultaneously demands absolute knowledge. That combination is designable: closed-loop reproduction with relative commands only. It is the only item design we are able to defend, and it is untested here.

6.3 The output channel cannot be closed

The model's output is text. It can write # running heading: 47 in the middle of the program and attend back over it at every subsequent token. If intermediate reasoning is forbidden in the output — strict parsing, turtle commands only — the model can still recompute the running total at each step by summing the turn values it has already emitted, because it can see them.

The structural problem

There is no configuration of this task that forces the computation into a non-textual channel. Every quantity the task requires is either readable locally from the image or recomputable by summing the model's own emitted tokens. This is a property of an architecture whose only output is text, and no item design changes it.

6.4 What survives

The probe cannot answer "do models think in images?" That question is not reachable from the outside by this route.

A narrower question remains measurable: do models have a perceptual shortcut that beats a text-strategy baseline? If a model solves closed-loop relative-only items better than a text-only strategy could predict — and better than its own performance on a matched non-spatial task of identical token cost — then something perceptual is doing work. This is falsifiable. It is also a considerably smaller claim than the one the design was built to support, and the distance between the two is worth stating explicitly rather than eliding.

7. Design rules for capability probes

The negative result is specific to turtle graphics. The framework is not. We state it as five rules, intended to be applied before any model is called.

7.1 Two-door validity

Test whether the item can be solved by a cheap program and by a small set of absolute actions. Guarding one door is insufficient; the second is often the cheaper bypass and leaves no trace in the output statistics.

7.2 Degeneracy is joint, not intrinsic

Report degeneracy as a function of tolerance, or the threshold becomes the finding. A binary pass/fail verdict conceals the fact that the experimenter selected the answer.

7.3 Prefer scaling over thresholds

"Does the required complexity grow with input length?" is robust to threshold choice. A single number at a single length is not. Where both are available, report the scaling behaviour as the primary result.

7.4 Floor-check the measurement

Feed the exact known answer back through the complete pipeline — metric, alignment, normalisation — and assert it scores zero. Four bugs in our own code were found this way, all of which made the result look better than it was (Appendix A). This check should run on every execution and fail loudly.

7.5 Ask what each required quantity costs to obtain

Enumerate the quantities the item requires the subject to know. For each, ask whether it is locally readable from the input, or recomputable from the subject's own output. If every quantity is one or the other, the item cannot isolate the mechanism it claims to measure, regardless of how difficult it is.

8. Threats to validity

8.1 Internal validity

The central threat is our own measurement code. Four bugs were found, all in the direction of flattering the conclusion, and all four were caught only by the floor check of §4.4. We regard the remaining risk as low but non-zero, and the floor check is included in the released harness for this reason.

8.2 Construct validity

The principal construct concern is precisely the subject of §6: the metric does not measure what it was designed to measure. This is not a residual threat but the paper's main result.

8.3 External validity

Nine shape families are not a survey. They were chosen to span analytic, periodic and random constructions rather than sampled from a population, and no claim is made about the distribution of shapes in any real task. The scaling test mitigates this for the degeneracy finding by reporting behaviour across four lengths per family, but the family selection remains a choice.

8.4 Conclusion validity

The fitter's search class bounds every "valid" verdict from below. Where we report a shape as valid, the correct reading is that no cheap program was found within a class of polynomials to degree 3 and periodic sequences to period 12. Section 5.4 gives a worked example of a family that escapes this class while being an obvious closed form.

8.5 Scope

No model was called. Every cost figure is derived from token accounting rather than measured spend, and every fidelity figure is a property of the analysis code rather than of any subject. This paper is an analysis of an instrument, not an experiment on models, and should be read as such.

9. Conclusion

We set out to build a probe for spatial reasoning out of turtle graphics, on the theory that imperative relative commands would force a model to hold spatial state. We tested the probe before using it, and it failed four ways.

Three of those failures are general to capability probes and are checkable in advance: items can be degenerate; degeneracy has more than one door; and the verdict depends on a threshold the experimenter chooses. We give a five-rule framework for catching all three before spending compute.

The fourth failure is specific and fatal. The quantities the task requires are either locally readable from the image or recomputable from the model's own output, so the task cannot force computation into a non-textual channel, and therefore cannot answer the question it was built to ask.

We publish the negative result because the alternative is to publish the version that looks good in a demonstration. Our flagship item — a spiral, which renders beautifully and reads as a serious geometric task — is the single most degenerate shape we tested. That is a general lesson about capability probes, and it is the reason the analysis is worth more than the benchmark would have been.

Appendix A: measurement bugs

Four bugs were found in our own measurement code during the course of this work. We report them because a negative result that cannot be reproduced is not a result, and because all four had made the headline finding look stronger than it was. Each was caught by the floor check of §4.4.

Table 7. Bugs found in the measurement pipeline, and their effect on the result.
BugEffect
Kinematics were derived from a path resampled to equal arc length. Resampling to equal arc length forces every segment to the same length by construction. The step-length sequence was erased and the test was silently measuring turn angles only.
heading -= turn instead of heading += turn in the integrator. Every candidate reproduction was compared against a mirror of the target. Because the metric is nearest-point, a mirrored smooth curve still scores well and the error does not announce itself.
Alignment returned a centred shape and scored it against an uncentred original. A constant translation error was injected into every measurement.
The rotation search was unseeded. For symmetric shapes the search locks onto the wrong symmetry axis — a five-point star is invariant under 72° rotations — and reports a large error for an exact reproduction.

The measured error of the flagship spiral was 3.03% before these fixes and 0.27% after. The headline finding was understated by a factor of eleven by bugs that made the instrument look better than it was.
